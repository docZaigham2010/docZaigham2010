Executive Summary

Markhor Capra falconeri, the world’s largest wild goat, is native to Central Asia. The flare-horned subspecies, Capra falconeri, known as the Pir Panjal or Kashmir markhor, occurs only in the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir in India. Because they occur near international borders, Markhor habitats are prone to livestock grazing and developmental activities, including roads, mining, high-tension power lines, and border fencing. Overall, there is consensus that their populations are declining and that sustained conservation efforts are needed to ensure their continued survival within their only distribution range in the Indian subcontinent.

The Markhor Recovery project, being implemented by the Wildlife Trust of India (WTI) since 2005, has been working in close collaboration with the Department of Wildlife Protection (DWP), Jammu and Kashmir (J&K) to ensure the long-term survival of the Kashmir Markhor (listed in Schedule I of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, and 'Near Threatened' under the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species (IUCN Red List 2014).

Overall in the last four years, WTI in partnership with the DWP, J&K and financial support from the Astral Foundation, carried out project activities specifically aiming at monitoring the Markhor population growth and recruitment rates in the Protected Areas (PAs), securing the Critical Markhor Habitats (CMHs) in the PAs, especially in the Pir Panjal range by reducing livestock grazing pressure, enhanced patrolling by trained and equipped frontline staff and local community members in the Kazinag and the Pir Panjal range, strengthening the capacity of law enforcement agencies, and reducing the dependency on forest resources and increasing support to the local communities in the Kazinag range by promoting alternative livelihood options in the fringe villages of the Kazinag National Park. In addition, we aimed to enhance the visibility and awareness of Markhor conservation efforts in J&K.

Over the past four years, Markhor conservation efforts in J&K by the Wildlife Trust of India (WTI) and the Department of Wildlife Protection (DWP), J&K, with support from the Astral Foundation, have achieved Markhor population growth estimated using scientifically robust monitoring methods, Critical Markhor Habitats (CMHs) protection in the PAs, strengthened capacity of the DWP frontline staff and enforcement agencies, and empowered local communities as custodians and key stakeholders in Markhor Conservation.

Key Achievements

  • An increase in Markhor population in the Kazinag range from 221 ± 12.5 individuals in 2023 to 227 ± 13.9 individuals in 2026.
  • A recruitment rate of 69.0% in the Kazinag Markhor population, indicating strong reproductive success and juvenile survival.
  • 2000+ km surveyed in Kazinag and 1400+ km surveyed in the Pir Panjal range.
  • 8% reduction in livestock population grazing in the Hirpora Wildlife Sanctuary (WLS).
  • 140+ DWP frontline staff trained and equipped for effective patrolling and wildlife monitoring.
  • A network of 44 Markhor Watchers established to strengthen community engagement in conservation initiatives.
  • Reduction in non-bonafide/illegal livestock grazing in the Hirpora WLS by 26% in 2025.
  • Ecotourism initiatives around the Kazinag National Park – A setup involving 6 Homestays and 22 nature guides established around the Kazinag National Park.
  • Public outreach involving 4000+ people, alongside international and national recognition of these conservation models by presenting the project and its impacts in over seven forums.

Way Forward

Building on the progress achieved so far, continued collaboration between the Wildlife Trust of India and the Department of Wildlife Protection, Jammu & Kashmir will remain essential for securing the long-term future of the Markhor in Jammu and Kashmir. Future efforts should focus on strengthening protection and monitoring in key habitats, particularly within Kazinag National Park and Hirpora Wildlife Sanctuary, while further reducing grazing pressure and mitigating emerging threats from infrastructure and human disturbance. Using advanced population monitoring methods, including thermal drones, camera traps, and radio-collars, would improve data quality and enable more informed conservation planning and implementation. Expanding community-based initiatives such as the Markhor Watcher network, promoting sustainable livelihood options through ecotourism, and enhancing scientific monitoring of populations and habitats will be critical. These integrated actions will help consolidate the gains made under the Markhor Recovery Project and ensure the sustained conservation of this flagship species across the landscape.

Background

The Markhor (Capra falconeri) – the world’s largest goat - is an iconic, Near Threatened mountain ungulate with ecological and cultural significance in the Kashmir Himalaya. In India, it occurs only in the Union Territory (UT) of Jammu and Kashmir (J&K). Like other ungulates in mountainous ecosystems, the species has been affected from livestock grazing, poaching, habitat fragmentation, and developmental pressures such as road construction and linear intrusions. Exposure to these anthropogenic pressures over an extended period has made them highly vulnerable to local extinction in parts of their distribution range in the UT. Of the two viable populations in J&K, the population in the Kazinag range has gradually recovered over the past two decades, while the same is not true for the population in Pir Panjal range, especially in the Hirpora Wildlife Sanctuary (WLS).

Following range-wide population assessment in J&K in 2004-05 (Bhatnagar et al., 2009), the Wildlife Trust of India has been consistently supporting conservation efforts through Markhor Recovery Project in collaboration with the Department of Wildlife Protection (DWP), J&K, with support from multiple funding partners.

Through the project, continued efforts have been made to improve the status of the species. The project monitored the Markhor population and its habitats; ensured reduced anthropogenic pressure on Critical Markhor Habitats (CMHs) in PAs; built the capacity of frontline staff and local youth to enhance protection and vigilance in Markhor habitats; identified suitable Markhor habitats outside PAs; and reduced local communities' dependence on Markhor habitats.

The report compiles the efforts and achievements towards Markhor conservation in the state over the last four years, largely supported by funding from the Astral Foundation.

1.1

WTI and DWP Partnership Towards Markhor Recovery in J&K

Over the past two decades, the Wildlife Trust of India, in close collaboration with the Department of Wildlife Protection, Jammu & Kashmir, has been actively involved in the recovery and conservation of the Markhor in the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir alongwith the local communities living in and around the Markhor habitats, both within and outside the PAs.

The collaboration began in 2004-05 with the first status survey (Bhatnagar et al. 2009), which mapped and monitored Markhor distribution across the region. Based on these early surveys, several conservation measures were undertaken, including the notification of Kazinag National Park in 2007 through the consolidation of Limber Wildlife Sanctuary, Lachipora Wildlife Sanctuary and Naganari Conservation Reserve. Additionally, around 150 km² was added to Hirpora Wildlife Sanctuary in 2007, and later, in 2012, Tatakutti Wildlife Sanctuary (66 km²) was notified following WTI’s documentation of Markhor in the area.

Joint efforts have contributed to reduced non-traditional livestock grazing in Hirpora WLS, leading to a decrease of nearly 3,000 livestock in recent years (2018-2025. Capacity development of frontline staff to improve wildlife monitoring, enforcement, and response to wildlife crime was another area in which WTI and DWP, J&K, worked together.

Community participation has been a key component of the initiative. Local youth have been organized into monitoring and vigilance teams known as ‘Markhor Watchers’, who assist in field surveys, track wildlife movements, and report illegal activities. Additionally, efforts have aimed to reduce fringe villages' dependence on forests by linking communities with government livelihood schemes and promoting ecotourism. With the support of the Department of Tourism, Jammu and Kashmir, an ecotourism society has been formed, with homestays and trained nature guides from fringe villages registered to promote sustainable livelihood opportunities.

Markhor Population Trend in the Kazinag Range

Markhor population estimates

We estimated the Markhor population in the Kazinag National Park, including Limber and Lachipora WLSs using a scientifically robust double-observer method in 2023 and 2026. We systematically walked 2000+ km in the park over the last four years. The population estimates generated in 2026 and 2023 were 227 ± 13.9 individuals and 221 ± 12.5 individuals, respectively. Also, the population recruitment rates suggest a healthy growing population in the Kazinag range with high female to male and female to kid ratio.

  • Covered a distance of 2000+ km in the Kazinag range – During the past four years, we have walked more than 2000 km on 12 permanently marked trails in the Kazinag range to monitor the Markhor population in the Kazinag National Park, including the Limber and Lachipora WLSs and the Naganari Conservation Reserve.
  • Population estimates show a stable trend – The 2026 population estimate of 227 ± 13.9 (95% CI: 213.1–240.9) indicates a broadly stable population compared with 2023 estimate of 221 ± 12.5 individuals and previous estimates. The estimated number of Markhor groups present in the area was 48, with a mean group size of 5.11 ± 0.62 (range 1-18). The population trend in the last two decades show a slow and steady growth from 133 individuals recoded in 2005 till 227 individuals in 2026 (Figure 2).
  • Assessed population growth – We recorded a male-to-female ratio of 33 males per 100 females and a high kid-to-adult female ratio of 69 kids per 100 adult females, indicating strong recruitment and suggesting a healthy and potentially increasing population.

Secured Critical Markhor Habitats (CMHs) in Protected Areas

Reducing livestock grazing pressure in CMHs between 2022 and 2026:

In the last four years (2022-26), the livestock grazing pressure in the Hirpora WLS has reduced by 8%; the non-bonafide herding practices have reduced by 26%; more than 185 transient herders have been deterred from unauthorised camping in and around the sanctuary.

  • Identified CMHs in Kazinag NP and Hirpora WLS using a robust framework – According to habitat suitability analysis, 50% and 18% of the total area in the Kazinag range are highly suitable and critical for markhor presence during the summer and winter seasons, respectively. In Hirpora WLS, only 14% and 11% of the area were highly suitable and critical for markhor presence during the summer and winter seasons, respectively.
  • Established anti-poaching and grazing checkposts – DWP and WTI established and supported grazing check posts at strategic locations in Hirpora WLS and verified herders' documents en route to their grazing pastures.
  • 185 transient herders were deterred from unauthorised entry in the Hirpora WLS – Establishment of anti-grazing camps by DWP and WTI, and strict surveillance at strategic entry points prevented transient livestock herders from illegally entering and camping in Hirpora WLS.
  • 8% reduction in livestock numbers and 26% reduction in non-bonafide herders in Hirpora WLS between 2021 and 2025 – The three-year surveys indicated an 8% decline in livestock numbers, i.e., from a baseline of 9,626 (average of 2022, 23, & 24) to 8,866 in 2025. The surveys also indicated that non-bonafide herding decreased by 26.38% in 2025 compared with the baseline (the average of the past three years, 2022-2024).

Reduced the Dependency of Local Communities on Markhor Habitats

Reduced dependency of local communities in fringe villages around Kazinag National Park:

The livelihood interventions introduced by WTI have enabled at least 32 households to earn a living from various sources. At the macroeconomic level, total revenue generated from the tailoring center and homestays (approximately Rs. 1,17,000 in the 2025 assessment period) may seem small, but in the context of rural fringe village economies, this value is significant.

This has also led more than 70% of local communities in the fringe villages in the Kazinag range to strongly support Markhor conservation in the area.

  • Trained and sensitized a group of 22 nature guides and six homestay owners through a series of training and workshops on hospitality, management, and first aid in the last three years.
  • 17 identified nature guides applied for registration as nature guides at the Kashmir Tourism Department.
  • Homestays in Kazinag hosting tourists – The six registered homestays are hosting tourists and earning revenue from the initiative.
  • 10 Markhor watchers working as paid field guides with various research institutions – Of the 20 Markhor watchers, ten are frequently involved and are paid for assisting the team and other researchers and research institutions during their fieldwork.
  • More than 70% of the local communities around Kazinag NP support Markhor conservation efforts – A questionnaire survey suggested that the local communities in and around the Kazinag NP support Markhor as well as wildlife conservation in the Kazinag range. Most of the responses were positive and supportive (>70-80%).

Awareness and Outreach for Markhor Conservation

Awareness and outreach programs:

The awareness among the local mass was enhanced by organizing various outreach and awareness programs, in which more than 4000+ people participated in a UT-level Wildlife Walkathon organized in wildlife week 2023; 200+ students participated in various awareness programs, and the work was represented in various national and international forums in the last four years.

  • Over 4,000 people participated in the UT-level Wildlife Walkathon organized to celebrate Wildlife Week in 2023 – As part of the outreach program, a Wildlife Walkathon was organized on the 2nd of October 2023, i.e., Wildlife Week, by the Department of Wildlife Protection, J&K, in collaboration with WTI. The Hon’ble Lieutenant Governor of J&K flagged off the walkathon, which was a significant success, with over 4,000 participants, including stakeholders such as the J&K Police, students, scholars, and faculty members from local schools, colleges, and Universities, as well as representatives from local NGOs.
  • 200+ school children participated in various wildlife awareness and outreach programs – Students from 10 government schools in the fringe villages of Kazinag and the Pir Panjal ranges participated in various events like nature walks, essay competitions, and painting competitions, organized on the occasions of Wildlife Week, International Day of the Markhor, and World Species Congress.
  • Presented the Markhor work at national and international forums – including the International Conference on ‘Wildlife Conservation: Emerging Scenario and Way Forward’, Indian Conference on Conservation (ICCON, 2024), Indian Wildlife Ecology Conference (IWEC), second international conference on Hangul and other threatened ungulate conservation (2IHUC, 2025), and 8th and 9th World Congress on Mountain Ungulate (WCMU) in Italy and Tajikistan.
